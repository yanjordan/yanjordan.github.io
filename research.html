Research

Nanomedicine is where my methods work is heading. A nanocarrier is hard to model for a simple reason: the properties that decide whether it works — self-assembly, drug loading, stability, release, interaction with membranes and proteins — live on different length and time scales, and no single method covers all of them. The three directions below are my answer to that problem: get the scales to talk to each other, use machine learning where exact physics is too slow, and let optimization decide which experiment to run next.

Physics-based modelling

1. Multiscale simulation of nanomedicine

Lipid nanoparticles, polymeric micelles and metal-organic frameworks each demand a different resolution. We build workflows that pass information between electronic structure theory (DFT), all-atom molecular dynamics, and coarse-grained / dissipative particle dynamics (DPD), so that formulation-level questions can be answered from physics: what does this carrier self-assemble into, where does the payload sit, and what makes it come out again?

  • DFT
  • all-atom MD
  • coarse-grained models
  • DPD
  • self-assembly
  • drug release

ML + first principles

2. Machine learning for physics-based simulation

Accurate quantum methods are too expensive for the system sizes and time scales that matter in drug delivery. In Yan et al., Nat. Commun. 2024 we replaced the quantum-mechanical bottleneck inside multiscale quantum refinement with a machine-learning potential, making reliable refinement of protein-drug complexes affordable. We keep pushing this line: ML potentials, learned physical descriptors (such as the local-distortion indices from D2AF), and surrogate models that preserve the physics while dropping the cost.

  • ML potentials
  • QM/MM acceleration
  • descriptors
  • surrogate models
  • Δ-learning

Closed-loop experimental design

3. Bayesian optimization for design and discovery

Designing a nanoformulation is a mixed-variable, multi-objective problem: continuous component ratios, categorical lipid or ligand identities, boolean process switches, and objectives that genuinely conflict — encapsulation efficiency against particle size against stability against toxicity. We develop Bayesian optimization tooling built for that setting: mixed search spaces, single- and multi-objective acquisition functions (including hypervolume-based qNEHVI), and a uniform suggest/observe interface that plugs into real experimental campaigns. The target is a closed loop — the model proposes the next batch, the bench returns the data, and the loop tightens.

  • Bayesian optimization
  • mixed variables
  • multi-objective
  • qNEHVI
  • active learning
  • self-driving lab

Foundations

The directions above stand on earlier work in multiscale quantum chemistry and quantum crystallography. Full details are on the publications page.

Quantum refinement of biomacromolecules

Refining protein and protein-drug structures against X-ray data while keeping the chemistry honest, using QM/MM and ONIOM-based multiscale models.

J. Chem. Theory Comput. 2021; Nat. Commun. 2024

Charge, spin and momentum densities

Joint refinement of complementary experiments (X-ray diffraction, polarized neutron diffraction, magnetic Compton scattering) to reconstruct the one-electron reduced density matrix, in both position and momentum space.

Phys. Rev. B 2017 (I & II); Acta Cryst. A 2018; J. Chem. Phys. 2018; IUCrJ 2019

Quantum tunnelling under external electric fields

How an applied electric field steers heavy-atom tunnelling, and what that means for reactivity and selectivity.

J. Phys. Chem. Lett. 2023

Local distortion analysis

An atom-resolved decomposition of distortion energy that turns the distortion/interaction model into a distortion map, and yields indices usable as ML descriptors.

Chem. Sci. 2025
